# Project Specification: Exercise Repetition Counting System

## 1. Problem Definition

We investigate whether pose-based or appearance-based visual representations
produce more reliable repetition counts from exercise video. The task is per-video
integer count prediction from a single monocular video with no additional sensor input.

**Research question:** Do pose-based and appearance-based representations differ in
their ability to count exercise repetitions from video, and does this difference vary
by exercise type?

**Motivation:**

Exercises present structurally distinct visual challenges. A squat is a vertical
lower-body movement; its dominant signal lies in knee flexion and hip displacement.
A push-up requires the body to be horizontal, substantially altering the visible
keypoint geometry relative to a standing subject. A pull-up is a vertical upper-body
pull whose signal quality is sensitive to camera angle and viewing distance.

These structural differences raise the question of whether a single visual
representation is sufficient across exercise types, or whether the optimal
representation varies with the exercise. We design the experimental pipeline to
investigate this question. The data viability analysis in Section 4.1 establishes
that RepCount / LLSP Part-A does not provide sufficient data to answer it
conclusively; we present our results as hypotheses for future investigation on a
viable dataset.

**Contributions:**

(i) An end-to-end pipeline for comparing pose-based and appearance-based temporal
models for exercise repetition counting on RepCount / LLSP Part-A, spanning
YOLO11n-pose extraction, feature engineering, TCN and Transformer training, and
offline routing evaluation.

(ii) A data viability framework with explicit, literature-grounded criteria for
minimum dataset requirements before model comparison is meaningful (Section 4.1).

(iii) A structured failure-mode analysis on RepCount generating testable hypotheses
about pose quality, label consistency, and representation adequacy across exercise
types (Section 8).

(iv) A squat runtime prototype demonstrating the full pipeline from raw video to
predicted repetition count, including both a rule-based finite state machine and a
learned TCN branch.

**Open questions surfaced by this work:**

- Does pose estimation quality degrade systematically across exercise types, and if
  so, is the dominant cause occlusion, normalization mismatch, or feature design
  choices?
- Can appearance-based features substitute for pose-based features when pose quality
  is reduced, and under what conditions does this substitution hold?
- What minimum per-exercise validation set size and label quality are required to
  distinguish pose-based from appearance-based approaches reliably?

pose sequences and frozen CNN appearance features — and construct a family of
exploratory temporal models across each track. The primary outputs are the
failure-mode hypotheses in Section 8, generated by reviewing cases where branches
performed poorly.

## 4.1 Data Viability Assessment

**Before training any model, a dataset must meet a minimum standard: is there enough
data, of sufficient quality, to produce trustworthy results?** This section answers
that question for RepCount and documents why it informs the research scope — and
why the conclusion was reachable before a single model was built.

---

### What viability means

To reliably compare two approaches to counting repetitions, sufficient evaluation
examples are required so that a genuine difference in performance is distinguishable
from random variation. With too few examples, one approach may appear superior simply
because it encountered a more favorable sample of videos — not because it is
fundamentally better.

Quality requirements must also be satisfied: annotations must be trustworthy (the
labeled rep count must correspond to what is visible in the video), and the margin
of error on measured results must be narrow enough to support actionable conclusions.

A dataset is considered viable for this project if it satisfies all three conditions:

| Condition | Minimum bar | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Enough evaluation videos per exercise | ≥ 50 | Below this threshold, apparent differences between approaches cannot be reliably distinguished from chance variation (Demšar, 2006; Bouthillier et al., 2021) |
| Margin of error on results | < 2 rep range | A repetition counter is meaningful only at ±1 rep precision; a wider margin indicates results are too uncertain to support architectural decisions |
| Trustworthy labels | Counts correspond to visible video content | When labels do not match observable video content, no approach can score correctly on those examples — the performance ceiling is unknown |

---

### What the data shows

#### Evaluation volume — observable at the data exploration stage

The number of evaluation videos per exercise is directly readable from the annotation
files, before any modeling work is undertaken:

| Exercise | Evaluation videos | Meets threshold (≥ 50)? |
|---|---:|---|
| squat | 16 | **No** |
| pull_up | 14 | **No** |
| push_up | 18 | **No** |

Fourteen to eighteen videos per exercise is insufficient to distinguish genuine
performance differences from random variation. A single atypical video — filmed from
an unusual angle or with an ambiguous number of repetitions — can meaningfully shift
the measured result. This is a data volume challenge, not a modeling one, and it was
identifiable before any model was trained.

#### Margins of error — confirmed after the first training round

After training, the measured error ranges confirmed what the video counts indicated.
An error range spanning 7 repetitions means the true performance of an approach could
plausibly fall anywhere from acceptable to poor — the point estimate alone is not a
reliable basis for decision-making.

| Exercise | Measured error range (95%) | Range width | Meets threshold (< 2)? |
|---|---|---:|---|
| squat | 1.1 to 3.3 reps | 2.2 | **No** |
| pull_up | 2.1 to 7.5 reps | 5.5 | **No** |
| push_up | 3.3 to 10.4 reps | 7.1 | **No** |

For pull-up and push-up, the ranges are wide enough that the approach performing best
on 14 videos may not hold on a larger, more representative sample.

#### Label consistency — confirmed at the hard-case review stage

Manual review of videos where model performance was poor revealed that some annotation
counts do not correspond to what is visible in the video. The proportion of affected
samples was not precisely quantified; however, the confirmed existence of this pattern
establishes that no approach can achieve a perfect score — the true ceiling is unknown.
Results cannot be interpreted in absolute terms under these conditions.

---

### The stopping point

| Condition | Result | When this was knowable |
|---|---|---|
| Enough evaluation videos | **Not met** | Data exploration — before any training |
| Margins of error sufficiently narrow | **Not met** | After the first training round |
| Labels verified as trustworthy | **Not met** | After manual video review |

**The data volume condition alone was sufficient to identify the limitation.** It was
readable from the annotation files at the data exploration stage, before any feature
extraction, model selection, or training was undertaken. In a production or consulting
context, this finding would inform a data strategy decision before further investment
was committed.

---

### Research value of the exploratory pipeline

This project began as an academic model-comparison exercise. The viability analysis
presented here is a retrospective reframing — applied after the fact to situate the
findings honestly within the research literature and to extract the methodological
contribution that survives the data limitation.

The exploratory model runs documented in Sections 5 and 7 were carried out to
characterize *how* this dataset behaves under different representation choices, and
*what challenges* would need to be addressed with a more suitable dataset. Rather
than performance claims — which the data cannot support — the results serve as
behavioral observations that informed the failure-mode hypotheses in Section 8.
Those hypotheses are the primary research contribution, and they lay the groundwork
for a more robust study when a viable dataset is available.

---

### What a viable dataset requires

Any future study of this task should satisfy the viability criteria above before
committing to modeling. The minimum requirements are:

- At least 50 evaluation videos per exercise in the evaluation split
- Annotation counts verified against visible video content, with independent
  annotator agreement measured
- Measured error ranges narrow enough to distinguish approaches (< 2 rep range)

The Countix dataset (~9,000 videos) is the most promising near-term candidate. It
was developed specifically because the repetition counting research community
recognized that smaller datasets could not support reliable conclusions (Dwibedi
et al., 2020). A per-exercise audit of Countix against the criteria above is the
recommended first step before any training is committed.

### References

> Standard reference for comparing ML methods: ranking on small test sets is

> Demonstrates that variance from data sampling alone can render apparent benchmark

**Dwibedi, D. et al. (2020).** Counting Out Time: Class Agnostic Video Repetition
Counting in the Wild. *CVPR 2020*.
> Introduced Countix (~9,000 videos) to enable reliable repetition counting

> smaller benchmarks are insufficient for robust conclusions.

## 5. Exploratory Pipeline

To generate comparison data for the failure-mode analysis in Section 8, we trained
a family of temporal models across both representation tracks: a rule-based finite
state machine baseline, a dedicated squat TCN, a shared pose TCN, an RGB TCN with
ResNet18 and ResNet50 backbones, a Transformer encoder, and multimodal late-fusion
ablations.

These runs were conducted to observe *how* the system fails on this dataset, not
to produce generalizable performance claims. The data volume constraints documented
in Section 4.1 preclude that interpretation. Detailed training configurations and
run artifacts are available in `artifacts/3_Modeling/training_outputs/`.

The behavioral outputs — per-exercise MAE, RMSE, and Within-1 observations — are
recorded in Section 7 as the evidence base that informed the hard-case review
selection and the failure-mode hypotheses.

## 6. Features

### Generic Normalized Pose (all exercises)

Derived from raw YOLO11n-pose output `[T, 51]`:

1. Mask keypoints with confidence below 0.25
2. Forward-fill then backward-fill missing values over time
3. Apply exponential moving average smoothing (α = 0.2)
4. Normalize coordinates relative to the torso (shoulders and hips as anchor)
5. Resample to fixed sequence length for model input

### Squat-Specific Engineered Features (15 active columns)

| Feature group | Columns |
|---|---|
| Knee angles | Left knee, right knee, average, flexion delta |
| Hip geometry | Hip angle, left/right/average vertical position |
| Vertical positions | Left/right/average knee vertical, left/right/average ankle vertical |
| Leg kinematics | Hip drop, leg extension, hip velocity |
| Quality metrics | Validity flag, confidence support |

The raw feature table contains 16 columns including `frame_idx`; the trained model
drops `frame_idx`, leaving 15 input dimensions.

### RGB Appearance Features

Frames are sampled uniformly from each video and passed through a frozen backbone:

- ResNet18: 512-dimensional feature vector per frame
- ResNet50: 2048-dimensional feature vector per frame

## 8. Dataset Failure Mode Analysis

We conduct a manual review of hard cases — videos where pipeline outputs deviate
most from ground truth — to characterize failure patterns in RepCount / LLSP Part-A.
We identify eleven failure categories spanning data collection design, annotation
quality, and model behavior. Given the small per-exercise sample sizes (n = 14–18),
we present these as hypotheses for systematic investigation on a larger dataset,
not as confirmed findings.

| Category | Description | Exercises primarily affected |
|---|---|---|
| Pose failure | YOLO tracker lost or sustained low confidence | All |
| Occlusion | Keypoints hidden by body orientation or framing | Push-up (body horizontal) |
| Visibility issues | Partial subject in frame; limbs cut off by camera | All |
| Camera viewpoint | Extreme angles or unfavorable orientation | Pull-up, squat |
| Side-view framing | Subject filmed from the side; depth axis collapses joint angles | Squat |
| Camera motion | Shaky or panning camera introduces spurious motion signal | All |
| Target selection | Multiple subjects present; incorrect subject tracked | Pull-up |
| Rep boundary ambiguity | Indistinct transition between repetitions | Squat, pull-up |
| Fast motion | Motion blur or frame drop causes temporal model to miss rep peaks | All |
| Label mismatch | Annotation count does not correspond to the visible video | All |
| Pose jitter | High-frequency keypoint noise triggers spurious rule-based transitions | Squat |

**Hypotheses generated by the review:**

Hard-case analysis of the pipeline outputs (n = 16 squat, 14 pull-up, 18 push-up)
produced the following observations. These are patterns in a small sample; they
generate hypotheses for future investigation, not conclusions.

- Push-up cases show lower average pose confidence and valid-ratio than squat cases,
  but 90% of frames still have valid pose detections. Pose quality is moderately
  degraded, not absent. Whether this degradation drives the higher error — or whether
  label ambiguity and representation mismatch are more significant factors — cannot
  be determined from this sample size.
- The most frequent issue tag for push-up cases is `both_models_struggle` (8 of 18),
  suggesting that neither pose nor appearance features resolve the underlying
  challenge on this dataset. Label ambiguity (`likely_semantic_or_definition_ambiguity`)
  appears in 7 of 18 push-up cases, indicating annotation quality as a confounding
  factor.
- `rgb_recovers_when_pose_is_weak` was tagged in 4 push-up cases, suggesting
  appearance features may help in specific conditions — but not consistently enough
  to attribute the push-up difficulty primarily to pose failure.
- Failure modes differ across exercises in character, which is consistent with the
  hypothesis that exercises present different challenges — but the sample is too small
  to confirm this as a systematic pattern.

**Implications for dataset design:** A viable dataset for this task would require
consistent annotation protocols with verified label quality, sufficient per-exercise
volume to detect systematic patterns, and documentation of camera placement and
framing conditions per exercise type.

## 10. Assumptions

- The exercise label is provided as input at inference time. Automatic exercise
  recognition represents a natural extension for future development.
- Each video contains a single primary subject. Multi-person scenes are handled by
  IoU-based tracking, which selects one dominant subject per sequence.
- Video quality is sufficient for YOLO11n-pose to produce at least some valid
  keyframe extractions. A zero-array fallback is in place for fully untracked
  sequences, maintaining a stable file contract for downstream stages.
- Raw video files and large generated arrays (`.npy`, `.npz`) are not tracked in
  the repository. See the Data Policy section of `README.md` for the full list.

## 11. Current Scope

The following items reflect the current scope of this research phase. Each
represents a well-defined opportunity for the next stage of development.

- The live runtime is squat-only; pull-up and push-up runtimes are a clear next step
- The routed multi-exercise system is validated offline; packaging it as a single
  production inference endpoint is straightforward once a viable dataset is available
- Exercise recognition at inference time has not yet been implemented; this is the
  most impactful architectural addition identified by this research
- Validation subsets are small (n = 16 squat, n = 14 pull-up, n = 18 push-up);
  results are presented as behavioral observations, consistent with Section 4.1
- Most GPU-heavy training stages were executed in Colab notebooks; a fully scripted
  pipeline is a natural reproducibility improvement
- The reserved test split has not been used; it remains available for a clean final
  evaluation once the pipeline is frozen

## 12. Opportunities for Future Development

Listed in priority order based on impact on the primary open questions.

| Priority | Item | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Viable dataset acquisition | The exercise-dependency hypothesis requires a larger, well-structured dataset to test definitively; Countix is the most accessible candidate |
| 2 | Test split evaluation | The reserved test split provides a clean final evaluation once the pipeline is frozen |
| 3 | Exercise recognition layer | Removes the inference-time assumption that the exercise label is known; the most impactful architectural addition |
| 4 | Scripted end-to-end pipeline | A fully scripted pipeline enables complete reproducibility independent of Colab |
| 5 | Quantitative failure analysis | Failure categories are currently identified qualitatively; per-category error rates would strengthen the dataset characterization |
| 6 | Multi-exercise runtime | Squat has a packaged runtime; extending this to pull-up and push-up is a well-defined engineering step |
| 7 | Target-person selection | Multi-person tracking is handled by a simple heuristic; a dedicated selection module would improve robustness |
| 8 | Data augmentation evaluation | Augmentation is implemented across all trainers but its effect on Within-1 for pull-up and push-up has not been assessed |
| 9 | Stronger temporal models | ST-GCN, BlockGCN, or SkeleTR are strong candidates once a viable dataset and cross-dataset results are in place |
| 10 | Video-native counting models | RepNet, TransRAC, ESCounts, or CountLLM become candidates if the research direction intentionally shifts away from the interpretable pose/RGB branch design |

### Recommended Long-Term Architecture

```text
video
  -> target person selection / tracking
  -> exercise recognition
  -> branch selection
  -> repetition counting
```

Counting is most reliable when it occurs after the system has identified the
relevant person and the exercise context. This is the primary architectural lesson
of the routing experiment, and it points toward a modular, sequentially composed
system as the right long-term design.
