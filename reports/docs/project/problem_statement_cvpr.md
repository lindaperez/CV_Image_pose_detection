# Problem Statement and Contributions

## CVPR-Style Research Framing

Human motion understanding has become a core problem in computer vision, supporting applications in fitness coaching, rehabilitation, sports analytics, remote health monitoring, and human-computer interaction. While much prior video understanding work focuses on recognizing what action is occurring, many practical human activities require a finer level of temporal reasoning: the system must estimate how many complete motion cycles occur, despite variation in camera viewpoint, motion speed, body scale, occlusion, execution style, and background context. Exercise repetition counting is a representative instance of this challenge. A reliable counter from ordinary monocular video would remove the need for wearable sensors or manual logging, but it must convert noisy frame-level visual observations into a stable count over a variable-length sequence.

This project studies exercise repetition counting from video, with emphasis on repeated fitness movements such as squats, pull-ups, and push-ups. The task is deceptively simple: given a video clip and an exercise category, predict the total number of completed repetitions. In practice, the visual evidence needed for counting differs substantially across exercises. Squats are strongly defined by lower-body joint geometry and vertical body displacement, making pose keypoints a natural representation. Push-ups often depend on body alignment, contact with the floor, and viewpoint-specific appearance cues that can be weakened or lost in sparse pose coordinates. Pull-ups introduce additional ambiguity because target-person selection, camera angle, bar visibility, and partial occlusion can alter both pose quality and apparent repetition boundaries. As a result, repetition counting is not only a temporal regression problem; it is also a representation-selection problem.

Existing repetition-counting pipelines and many baseline approaches tend to assume that a single representation or architecture can generalize across heterogeneous repeated actions. RGB-based video models preserve appearance and scene context, but can overfit to background and dataset bias when data is limited. Pose-based models suppress background clutter and expose interpretable body motion, but they can fail when keypoints are noisy, occluded, or insufficient to capture the exercise-specific visual signal. Transformer and temporal convolutional models can improve temporal aggregation, yet architecture changes alone do not resolve cases where the input representation is mismatched to the exercise. Similarly, naive multimodal fusion may add complexity without improving discrete counting reliability. The resulting gap is that prior-style generic pipelines often report one overall model choice, while practical exercise counting requires understanding which modality fails, why it fails, and whether another representation is more defensible for that exercise.

We address this gap through an exercise-dependent repetition-counting pipeline that explicitly compares pose, RGB, multimodal, and routed counting strategies before selecting the final branch for each exercise. The system begins with annotation inspection and cleaning to convert noisy exercise-video metadata into a reproducible modeling contract. It then extracts YOLO-based human pose sequences, constructs both generic normalized pose sequences and squat-specific engineered pose features, and trains temporal counting models including finite-state baselines, TCN counters, RGB feature counters, transformer variants, and late-fusion models. Rather than forcing all exercise categories through one universal counter, the final design routes each supported exercise to the representation that is best supported by validation evidence: a dedicated pose-based TCN for squats, a pose-based route for pull-ups based on the practical accuracy tradeoff, and an RGB-based route for push-ups.

The research objective is therefore to build and evaluate a practical exercise-counting framework that answers three questions: first, when is pose sufficient for counting repetitions; second, when does RGB appearance provide information that pose discards; and third, how can these findings be converted into a usable counting architecture without overclaiming a universal solution? This framing makes the project's main contribution not a single new model block, but an evidence-driven architecture map for exercise repetition counting under realistic video noise.

## Key Contributions

1. We present a reproducible end-to-end pipeline for exercise repetition counting, covering dataset inspection, annotation cleaning, pose extraction, feature construction, temporal model training, evaluation, error analysis, and runtime demonstration.

2. We provide a controlled comparison of representation families for exercise counting, including squat-specific pose features, generic pose sequences, RGB frame features, pose transformers, weak density-style pose counting, and multimodal late fusion.

3. We show that repetition-counting performance is exercise-dependent rather than governed by a universal best modality: the strongest squat branch is a dedicated pose TCN, push-up benefits more from RGB features, and pull-up remains sensitive to viewpoint, target selection, and metric tradeoffs.

4. We document negative and inconclusive results as part of the research finding, including cases where stronger RGB backbones, generic pose transformers, keypoint weighting, density supervision, or simple multimodal fusion did not produce a robust improvement.

5. We introduce a hard-case review layer that connects quantitative errors to qualitative failure modes such as pose failure, camera viewpoint, target-person ambiguity, and repetition-boundary ambiguity.

6. We translate the empirical findings into an exercise-dependent routed counting prototype, including an offline squat counter and live squat demonstration, while clearly separating research-grade validation from production deployment claims.

Overall, this project frames exercise repetition counting as a modality- and exercise-aware video understanding problem. The central claim is that robust counting should not be built around a single assumed representation; it should choose the representation according to the visual and biomechanical structure of the target exercise, supported by validation evidence and error analysis.
